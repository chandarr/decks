## Purpose

Convert the India proposition from a country turnaround into a global competitive strategy.

The mechanism: India's engineering cost and its deployment diversity, funded by RaaS, produce proprietary manipulation and deployment data at the lowest cost per unit available anywhere. That data is fine-tuned onto KUKA's own certified hardware and sold at premium margin into the West, Japan and the Americas — which funds more deployment, and the loop tightens. The output is not an India business. It is a **repeatable playbook** that transfers to every geography sharing India's manufacturing profile, with models that arrive warm rather than cold.

Two objections are answered on the screen rather than left for the room:
- **RaaS means owning reliability risk.** True — and that is precisely why RaaS favours KUKA. The risk that kills a challenger is the moat for an incumbent with decades of industrial MTBF.
- **Physical AI may not mature on schedule.** Also true — and the downside is bounded, because the India engineering base is justified on cost arbitrage alone regardless of timing.

The one idea: *India can be the engine of KUKA's global position, not merely a market for its current products — and the downside of trying is a competitive India business and a low-cost engineering base.*

## Notes

- **Why this screen carries the mandate.** Everything before it argues that KUKA should fix India. This screen argues that fixing India *is* KUKA's global strategy — because the output is a repeatable mechanism, not a country P&L. That distinction is the difference between a country-manager brief and a global-strategy brief, and it is the specific thing the author is bidding for. If this screen reads as an India plan, it has failed.
- **The RaaS inversion is the sharpest move in the deck.** Naming the risk before the CEO does, then showing that the risk selects *for* KUKA and *against* new entrants, converts the most obvious objection into a competitive argument. Do not soften it into a caveat and do not bury it — it needs its own beat and its own visual treatment.
- **The asymmetry band is what makes the proposition approvable.** Frame it as bounded downside and uncapped upside, never as "negligible risk" — the second reads as a pitch and invites scepticism, while the first is analysis and survives scrutiny. The downside line must state a genuinely acceptable outcome, because that is the whole argument: the India engineering base is justified on cost arbitrage alone, whatever physical AI does.
- **Replication belongs here, not on screen 14.** Screen 14 is India-specific execution (Bengaluru, named partners), and putting the geographies there would read as scope creep on a country plan. Here it is structural — the flywheel produces a transferable playbook. This is the beat that makes the deck a global-strategy document.
- **Cross-screen dependencies:**
  - Node 3 closes the hollow ring on **screen 05** and answers row 3 of **screen 11**. The task files for both flag this; the callback should be recognisable without being labelled.
  - RaaS was introduced on **screen 12** as a Layer 1 commercial mechanism. Its data function is revealed here for the first time — the two-stage reveal is deliberate.
  - `low volume, high mix` must match the terminology used on screens 03 and 12 exactly.
  - The premium-margin node connects to **screen 15**'s closing argument. Do not preview that.
- **Verification flags** (carry into `VERIFY-BEFORE-SENDING.md`): the cost-arbitrage claim is directionally solid but has no single defensible multiple — keep it qualitative. Node 4's asset claims are the same ones flagged on screens 05 and 08 and are verified once for all three. The replication claim rests on those four geographies sharing India's manufacturing profile, which is an author assessment and should be presented as such if challenged.
